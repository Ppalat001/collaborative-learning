AI-Supported Collaborative Learning

A plain-English guide to how the website works, what it measures, and why the measurements are valid.

1.The one-minute version

“I built a website where small groups of students (2–4) solve 10 short tasks together. They discuss in a group chat, and if they get stuck they can ask an AI for one type of help, such as a hint, a guiding question, an example or feedback. The AI is designed to support their thinking, not do the work for them. The site records what the group writes, what help they asked for and why, who contributed, and how each student felt. At the end, I measure learning, answer quality, collaboration and dependence on AI.”

2.Purpose of the study

Main question: How does AI support, chosen by the group itself, affect how students learn and work together, without the AI taking over the task?

Sub-questions

#TopicQuestion
1LearningDo students know more after the activity?
2QualityHow good are the group answers, and does that relate to the type of AI help used?
3Help-seekingWhat help do groups choose, and why do they ask for it?
4CollaborationDoes AI help groups discuss, stay focused, include everyone and resolve disagreement?
5Autonomy and overdependenceDo students stay in control, or do they lean on the AI and copy it?
Why it matters: AI tools like ChatGPT can easily give full answers, which may stop students from thinking. This study tests whether graded, student-chosen support (giving the least help needed) keeps students active and learning.

3.How the website works, step by step

Start

  1. The researcher creates a session and gets a code (for example GROUP-RBKF).
  2. Students join on their own devices with the code and their name. Each student is given a hidden anonymous ID for the data.
  3. The group gives consent.
  4. Each student takes a pre-test (8 questions on collaborative learning and AI support) on their own.

For each of the 10 scenarios

#ScenarioType of thinking
1Explaining a ConceptExplain
2Improving a Weak AnswerExpand an answer
3Short Case StudyCase study

9Designing a ProcessCompare solutions

Step 1 — Discuss (group chat)

  • Students talk in a live chat.
  • Each message shows the name, the time and a colour per student, so it's clear who said what.

Step 2 — AI help (optional, once per scenario, shared by the whole group)

  1. The group says why it needs help: stuck / not sure if correct / need an example / disagree / need a better explanation / want feedback / no help needed.
  2. The group picks one type of help:
LevelType of helpMeaning
0No assistanceWork alone
1 · minimalHint, guiding questionA small push
2 · moderateExplanation, example, feedbackMore guidance
3 · directFull expert answerThe complete answer, with extra conditions (see below)
—Summary, task splitting, conflict helpHelps the teamwork, not the content
  1. The choice is a proposal, not a decision. It appears on every member's screen as “Maria proposed Small hint (Level 1) because: We are stuck”.
  2. Another member must agree. Any other member clicks Agree and ask the AI, or anyone clicks Cancel so the group can choose again. The proposer cannot agree to their own proposal (except in a one-person group).
  3. Only after agreement, the AI reads the chat and replies. Everyone sees the same response, labelled “Proposed by Maria · agreed by John”.

Example: how agreement works

MomentMaria's screenJohn's screen
1. Chat“Should we ask for a hint?” — “Yes, a hint sounds good.”
2. Maria proposesPicks “We are stuck” + “Small hint”. Sees Waiting for another group member to agree and a Withdraw button.Instantly sees the yellow box: Maria proposed Small hint… Do you agree? with Agree and Cancel.

Step 3 — Own words (only after a full expert answer)

  • The group must explain the AI's answer in their own words (at least 15 words). This prevents copying.

Final answer

  • The group writes one shared answer together, with live co-editing.

Individual rating

  • Each student rates the scenario separately: whether the help was useful, whether it was too little, about right or too much, how the teamwork went, and whether they felt in control.

End

  1. Each student takes a post-test (8 questions, matched one-to-one with the pre-test concepts).
  2. Each student answers 6 open questions about their experience.
  3. The researcher scores the answers with a rubric (blind) and downloads the data as spreadsheets.

4.How the AI is kept as a “helper, not a doer”

This is a key design argument for your professor:

One request per scenario

The AI can't be used again and again.

Propose and agree

No single student can call the AI alone. A second member must agree, so asking for help is a real group decision.

Strict instructions per help type

For example, a “hint” must never contain the answer.

Required help reason

The group must give a reason before asking, which makes them think about what they actually need.

Own-words explanation

After a full answer, students have to process it in their own words.

Least help first

Lower levels come first in the list, nudging groups toward the least help needed.

5.How the evaluation will happen

Data is collected at three levels:

LevelWhat is collectedWhen
StudentPre/post tests, ratings per scenario, open questions, individual contributionsStart, after each scenario, end
Group × scenarioChat, help reason, help type and level, AI response, final answer, timingsDuring each scenario
ResearcherRubric scores of the final answersAfter the study

Evaluation process

  1. Run a pilot with 1–2 groups to fix problems, then delete the pilot data.
  2. Run the study with several groups.
  3. Score the final answers blind, ideally with two raters on a sample.
  4. Download the CSV files from /admin.
  5. Analyse the numbers (statistics) and the open answers (themes).
  6. Compare and combine: check whether what students said matches what they did.

QuestionMethod

8.Why the measurements are valid

You can make five simple arguments to your professor:

1. Triangulation

Each key concept is measured in at least two ways:

  • Overdependence = what students say (rating) + what they did (AI overlap) + the result (rubric).
  • Collaboration = what students say (rating) + what they did (contribution logs).

When the sources agree, the conclusion is strong.

2. Objective behaviour data

Messages, typing, timings and text overlap are recorded automatically, so they don't rely on memory or honesty.

3. Individual and group data are separated

Tests, ratings and contributions are recorded per student, not assumed from the group answer.

4. Reduced bias

  • Blind rubric scoring: the rater doesn't know which help was used.
  • Anonymous IDs encourage honest answers.
  • AI questions are skipped when no AI was used, and “Not applicable” is offered where it fits.

5. Standard, recognised instruments

  • Pre/post testing, Likert scales and analytic rubrics are common in education research.
  • The support levels follow scaffolding theory (Wood, Bruner & Ross; Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development): give the minimum help needed.
  • Aligned content: the pre-test, the 10 scenarios and the post-test all focus on collaborative learning and AI support, so the tests measure what students actually practised.
  • Matched test forms: each pre-test question has a post-test partner on the same concept. Correct answers are spread evenly across A–D, so guessing one letter doesn't inflate scores.
Consistency: every group gets the same 10 scenarios, options and steps. Only their choices differ, and those choices are what you study.

9.Limitations (say these before your professor asks)

  • Groups choose their own help, so this is not a randomised experiment. You can show relationships, not strict cause and effect.
  • The pre/post tests are short (8 questions each). The two versions are matched by concept but use different wording, so they may not be exactly equal in difficulty. Pilot them first and check item difficulty.
  • Text overlap detects copied wording, not copied ideas.
  • If groups sit together and talk out loud, the chat misses that discussion. Ask them to use the chat as the main channel.
  • A small sample means the results are mainly descriptive and exploratory.
  • AI responses can vary. Note the model used (gpt-4o-mini), which is recorded in the data.

10.Summary slide

Purpose: Study how group-chosen AI scaffolding affects learning, collaboration and autonomy.

Design: Groups of 2–4 students, 10 scenarios, each with chat → optional AI help (10 types, levels 0–3) → shared answer → individual rating. Pre-test before, post-test and open questions after.

Safeguards: One AI request per scenario, a required help reason, strict AI rules, an own-words explanation after full answers.

Metrics: Learning gain, rubric quality, help type, level and reason, helpfulness, participation balance, collaboration ratings, AI overlap, autonomy ratings, open answers.

Validity: Triangulation of self-report and behaviour, automatic logs, individual-level data, blind scoring, standard instruments.

Limitations: Not randomised, short tests, small sample. Findings are exploratory associations.
